# Drawing rules

These 28 rules (A to E), the ten rules of Nature mode (F, default for figures for papers) and the 18 rules of model schematics and anti-overplating (G) decide how a Molecullage figure is drawn. Each one can be checked on the finished figure, by the QA script or by eye, and each says what goes wrong when it is broken. Sizes are for a figure 178 mm wide; at 89 mm, halve the widths and keep the type sizes. The numeric limits are close to values measured on a set of published antibody-design figures. Treat them as a guide.

## A. What a figure is made of

1. Give the figure one message, and state it in the first sentence of the caption in 20 words or fewer. Let one real object (one structure or one sequence) run through the figure in one orientation and one colour code. Put a second object in a separate panel. When two objects share a panel, the reader takes them for one, and a colour no longer means one thing.
2. Draw what flows between stages. Every box has a drawn object beside it, and two boxes in a row always have an object between them. Show an input as a picture of the input, not as a noun in a box. A row of named boxes tells the reader only the order of the steps; the objects between them show what each step does.
3. Use two kinds of rectangle only. A state card holds a picture, a tensor or a table. An operation box names a module and shows its input and output. Remove any rectangle that is neither. Keep to at most 8 boxes per panel and 12 words per box. Any other rectangle looks like content, and the reader looks for a meaning that is not there.
4. Before you draw, say what the picture would show in one sentence of 20 words. If nothing is lost, use a table or a list in the caption instead. A chain of six or fewer named steps with no branch, loop or drawn object always fails this test. Items set against four or more attributes are a table. A figure that fails this test takes space and shows nothing that the sentence or the table does not.
5. Draw missing data as a gap in the object itself: a dashed outline in place, or empty cells in a sequence strip, with the same fill for every mask in the paper. Draw noise as the real object degraded (dots), not as an icon. An icon for "missing" or "noise" is one more symbol to learn; a gap in the object needs no key.
6. Draw tensors as small fixed shapes. A pair map is a tile, a sequence is a strip, a batch is a stack of three offset cards, and a repeat is a count on the block ("x N"). The long side is at least 12 mm. Colour marks the data type; modules are grey or one tool colour. Expand one block per figure at most. When shapes change from tensor to tensor, or every block is expanded, the reader cannot tell data from modules.
7. Write time and noise as values. Every state of a trajectory carries its value as plain text and sits under one labelled arrow. A sweep is a strip with the value above or below each frame. Show at most five frames plus an ellipsis. Without the values the reader cannot tell the frames apart, and more frames only make each one smaller.
8. Give a concept that has no shape of its own computed toy data: 8 to 12 points or a small density, one toy per plate. A maths panel draws the quantity, not its symbol, and holds at most two equations. A symbol alone shows the reader nothing that the text does not already say.
9. Show evidence as a matrix. Rows are designs or conditions, columns are kinds of evidence, every row uses the same drawing, and the headers appear once. Name controls, thresholds and reference lines inside the data panel. Put the direction ("lower is better") in the title. When rows are drawn differently, the reader cannot compare them.
10. Show status in one way only. Implemented is a solid fill; designed or proposed is white and dashed. Do not add a second code in dash style or tint. Line style encodes data type, with at most three styles and the key inside the figure. When status is coded twice, the reader looks for a second meaning.

## B. Structure renders

11. Render structures in flat colour with a dark silhouette: no shading, no specular reflection, no cast shadow (ChimeraX `lighting flat`, specular 0, silhouettes of about 3 px at 2400 px wide). Use one background and at most four hues per render. Colour chains in family tints (binder rose, light chain periwinkle, antigen mint, epitope apricot), and loops in the dark tone of their chain. Draw a noise series as spheres at C-alpha positions; the last frame adds the real cartoon as a transparent tint. Shading changes the colour of a chain across the render, so the chain no longer matches its key or its text label.
12. A render that is the subject of the figure is at least 45 mm wide. In a row of three or four, each is at least 30 mm. A state in a process strip is at least 20 mm, and is allowed only where a larger render of the same object exists. Under 15 mm a render is a pictogram and makes no claim. The render fills at least 80 % of its cell. A smaller render cannot show the loops and residues that its labels point to.
13. Keep the viewpoint fixed across a row: antigen on the left, binder on the right, loop 3 facing the antigen. A close-up is an inset of at least 25 % of the figure width, with leaders to real residues and side chains as sticks. When the viewpoint changes between panels, the same object looks like a different one.
14. Put the label of every render in the same place, in at most three lines: a role or identifier, then one or two metrics, at least 7 pt. Put loop names at the loop tips, with straight leaders that end on a real residue. Set role colours once and use them in text labels too, so that the legend is one row or absent. When labels move from render to render, the reader has to search for them.
15. Say in the figure what every picture is: a deposited structure (PDB id), a decoy pose, an interpolation path or a toy example. The claim-boundary line (rule 20) carries this. Entry, chains, numbering, atom selection and cut-off go in the notes file. A reader who cannot tell a deposited structure from a toy will take the toy for data.

## C. Layout and type

16. Use Arial or Helvetica only, as live text in the PDF. Write maths in Arial italic with real sub- and superscripts (`f.rich`, `MS()` in the kit). Replace any symbol that falls back to another font, such as the tilde operator, until the PDF font list holds Arial only. A fallback font changes the letter shapes in the middle of a formula.
17. Panel letters are 9 pt bold lower case. Titles and object names are 7.5 pt bold. Labels are 7 pt. Secondary text and tick labels are 6.5 pt, and 6.5 pt is the floor for everything, including maths scripts (maths is 8.4 pt, and scripts at 78 % come to 6.5 pt). Use sentence case and the ink colour; use a role colour only for a label that names the role. Text below the floor is hard to read in print, and a role colour on an unrelated label suggests a link that does not exist.
18. Keep an outer margin of 1.5 mm and panel gaps of 2 to 4 mm. Everything in a column shares its edge: panel letter, image, card and text start at the same x. Labels sit in aligned rows and columns, leaders are straight, and the baselines of repeated items match. Edges that are almost aligned look like errors.
19. White space has a budget. In a grid of 20 columns and 12 rows, at most 25 % of the cells are empty. The longest empty band across the full width is at most 4 % of the height. The largest empty rectangle is at most 6 % of the area. Ink (pixels darker than 235) covers at least 9 %. Some empty space carries meaning and is allowed: the run of an arrow, a gutter, a dashed gap, an encoded quantity. Strict mode uses tighter limits: ink 20 %, cells 20 %, band 2.5 %, blank 4 %. A large empty area leaves less room for the content, which then needs smaller pictures and smaller type.
20. Use at most 130 words in a schematic and 90 in a plate, 6 per box or label, and 3 per arrow. Add one footnote line of 14 words or fewer, the claim boundary: what is real data and what is illustration. Everything else goes in the caption. Every block has a name; when its job is not obvious, it has one plain sub-line. Write sets of links as lists ("A1, A3"), never as ranges. A figure with too many words is slow to read, and one with too few cannot be read without its caption. A range claims members that may not be in the set.
21. Use an icon only when it stands for something: a real object, a number or a state. Use at most two per figure. Do not put a gear, a shield or scales on every box. An icon on every box adds ink and no information, and the reader still tries to decode it.
22. Use at most four hue families per figure, plus grey, and give each colour one meaning across all figures. Never use colour alone: every distinction also has text, a position or a shape. Text has a contrast of at least 4.5:1. Never make red against green the only difference. Readers with red-green colour blindness can see such a pair as one colour, and a greyscale print loses any difference carried by hue alone.
23. Do not use images from generative models. Many journals do not permit them. Do not put a generated sketch into a deliverable either. A generated picture can show detail that no data supports.

## D. Checks before a figure is used

24. `qa/qa_fig.py` measures ink, empty cells, empty band, blank rectangle, type size, render widths, words, fonts, pages and width on the built PNG and PDF. Every check passes, or the notes file gives the reason it does not apply. Without a written reason, the next person who builds the figure cannot tell a deliberate exception from a fault.
25. Before a figure is used, it gets three reads. The first is an overclaim read: an independent reader asks what a reviewer could say this picture proves that the work has not shown. The second is a blind read: a reader who sees only the PNG says what it shows. The third is the author's own look. Authors do not certify their own figures, because they know what a figure should show and see that in it.

## E. Schematics read by someone outside the field

These three rules came from a thesis, where the first block schematics were called "very good design, but non-intuitive" and "lacking schematics and representative thumbnails". A reader who does not know the field needs to be told what the system is for and shown one real example of what each block holds.

26. Say in one line at the top of a schematic what the drawn system is for, in plain words: at most 14 words, 7.5 to 8 pt bold. Write "proposed" when the system is a design. Without it a reader sees clean boxes and arrows and cannot tell what they serve. The line claims no more than the claim-boundary line allows.
27. Put one real example object in each block that matters: a thumbnail 12 to 22 mm wide (a structure render, a small matrix, or a mini plot made from the project's own data), with one frame style and the same place in every block, and its source (file, entry id, data file) in the notes. A thumbnail replaces words, so the word budget of rule 20 still holds. Structure renders must also pass the render-width check, so plan the page for it; the page then grows in height.
28. A thumbnail never shows a result for a block that has not run. On a designed (white, dashed) block it shows an input or an intended object, drawn grey. Say on the picture or in the key that it is an example, name a stand-in system ("mi3", "toy square"), and call a fragment a fragment ("part of MS2, 5TC1"). The status key says what solid and dashed mean, and a picture does not use dashed strokes for content, because the reader then cannot tell a drawn dash from the status dash. Readers who did not know the figure mistook full-colour pictures on solid blocks for outputs until the key said otherwise.

## F. Nature mode: draw the data, not boxes

Rules 29 to 38 make a figure read as a figure from a paper. Full text, the table "choose the form from the message" and the checks: `docs/nature-mode.md`. A page that is a real process diagram says so in `molecullage.json` (`standards.mode: "diagram"`) and in its notes file.

29. Form follows message: a number, a trend, a comparison, a ranking, shares and counts get axes and marks; boxes only for a process with real objects. At most one panel of a figure is a diagram. Rectangles that hold words cover at most 25 % of the area (QA mark `boxes`). A figure of boxes tells the order of steps and no number, so it reads as a slide.
30. Every axis names its quantity and unit; the panel title gives the direction; a threshold is a line or a band named in place.
31. Show the data: the points when a group has 30 values or fewer, a stated interval otherwise. Never draw a mean as a bare bar.
32. Repeated comparisons are small multiples on one axis, one scale and one order.
33. Label in place; a legend only for what cannot carry a label; no text over a shaded area or an image.
34. Panel letters 8 pt bold lower case; other text 7 pt or smaller; floor 6.5 pt; one sans-serif font; lines 0.25 to 1 pt (Nature).
35. Width 89 mm, 120 to 136 mm or 183 mm; depth at most 247 mm (Nature). Old figures at 178 mm stay valid.
36. Vector PDF with live text; the PNG is for preview. Do not rasterise plots or text.
37. One meaning per colour across the figure set; at most four hue families; sequential ramps in one hue; diverging only around a meaningful zero; no rainbow; check with `palette_check`.
38. Source, number of items and a mark for every computed value stand on the figure. Never invent a curve, an error bar or a p-value.

## G. Model and method schematics, and anti-overplating

Rules 39 to 56 come from reading published machine-learning and chemistry figures in JCTC, JCIM, JACS, Nature Communications, Angewandte Chemie and Chemical Science, and from fixing overplated figures (2026-11-01 to 2026-11-07).

39. Look at real papers before you draw. Read three to five published figures of the same kind and list in your notes what they show and what they leave out. A figure drawn from memory alone drifts into boxes.
40. Boxes are for operations only. Molecules, point clouds, graphs, tensors, tokens and outputs sit on the page or in pale tint panels with no border. Never put a box inside a box (QA mark `overplate`). When everything sits in a box, the reader cannot tell an object from a step.
41. Keep at most five groups on the page. Each group is a pale tint panel with no frame, no stage strip, no step badge and no corner tag. Every extra frame or badge steals space from the science.
42. Write the title on the page. Leave out banner bars, paper tags, status keys and footers; put one short sentence at the bottom edge for the caption or claim boundary.
43. Show a neural or mathematical layer by its shape. Draw an encoder or decoder as a funnel or trapezoid, attention as stacked planes, a message-passing step as nodes and edges on the molecule, and an energy term as a curve or surface.
44. Write a formula once, in the place where it acts. Keep variable definitions in the caption. Repeating the same equation in a card and on an arrow wastes space.
45. Show the input and the output at real size. In a figure 178 mm wide, the input molecule or complex and the output plot or structure are each at least 35 mm wide.
46. Use one arrow between two groups. Each arrow is an orthogonal line or a smooth cubic curve. Its label sits beside the line with a 1.5 mm gap. No arrow crosses another arrow or a text label.
47. Keep to at most 80 words on a model schematic. Each module name is one to three words, and each note is one short line of two to four words. Start every label and note with a capital letter.
48. Spell out every uncommon abbreviation on the figure or in the caption. Never leave bare shorthand such as "2e", "14 res" or "GBDT" unexplained.
49. Write chemical formulas with upright element symbols, subscripts and superscript charges. Draw the organic-phase overbar over the species only, never over stoichiometric coefficients or arrows.
50. Sample the palette from a real render or reference figure with `palette_sample` and check it with `palette_check`, or use the native project palette. Do not apply default navy and amber fills or Molecullage palette G unless requested.
51. Draw 2D chemical structures with RDKit `MolDraw2D` in ACS-1996 mode from a checked SMILES string. Never hand-code atom coordinates or bonds in SVG.
52. Render 3D molecular complexes in ChimeraX, SAMSON or Blender with Molecular Nodes from verified coordinates, with soft studio or flat journal lighting and a clear camera. Never draw a protein or complex as a circle-and-stick cartoon or C-alpha bead cloud.
53. Resolve every 2D icon, 3D model, HDRI, datasheet, structure and paper through the consolidated library (`studio/library/sources.json` and `studio/library/references.json`) before building.
54. In a comparison matrix, put each cell's item count at the top-left of the cell, put the row total on the right of the row, and keep one graphic grammar across all cells.
55. No divider, lifeline or connector may pass through a text label. Leave a clear gap or break the line around the label.
56. For journal PDF submission, use Arial or Helvetica so text embeds as scalable vector fonts. If a slide font embeds as Type3 bitmaps in PDF, switch to Arial for the print PDF.
57. When a structure is too small for 6.5 pt atom letters, draw its atoms as dots in the element colours of the figure's key. Take the dot positions from the same RDKit coordinates in every frame.
58. Do not join two items with a middle dot or a bullet point, such as "TBP · U/Pu". Write the link as a word: "TBP for U/Pu".
59. Show the hardest separation the model must make. Render each side from a crystal structure. Where one side has no crystal, give its measured separation factor with conditions and source. Never draw a modelled geometry as if it were measured.
60. Draw a deep-learning model as published ML-potential figures do (UMA arXiv 2506.23971, DPA3 arXiv 2506.01686, NequIP 10.1038/s41467-022-29939-5). Zoom from system to network to block to operation in lettered steps. Use rounded boxes with short labels, a ⊕ for each residual skip, inputs entering from the side, and a dashed arrow for derived outputs. Colour blocks in greys and tints, so that operation colours never repeat the metal-group colours.
61. Keep every mark apart. No label, arrow or dot may touch another mark. In a 3D render, choose the camera and the atoms shown so that no atom hides the metal or its donor atoms.
62. Put no dividing line, frame or bar between blocks on a slide. Separate blocks with white space only. End a cropped render in a soft fade, so that its edge does not read as a frame. A floor shadow that ends at the picture edge is a frame too: render on a transparent film with no ground. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
63. Show every part of a kit, from the first part the user touches to the last. Place the parts in the order of use, left to right. A slide that shows two parts of five hides how the kit works. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
64. Give a product render a three-quarter view from above, as a product photograph has. Do not show a flat side view or a cut as the main picture; a cut is a second picture. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
65. Compose a deck scene in the Molecullage deck engine (a film file, 1920 by 1080). Use the theme of the venue that the owner names. For HSE University the theme is `hse`: HSE Sans, the sign, the deck name and the slide number at the top, the title in HSE blue. Use no venue mark when the owner names no venue. (Owner, 2026-11-09, second correction.)
66. Put no subtitle under the title of a slide. Put no status or foot line on a slide, such as "prototype" or a model version. Say the status in the narration and write it in the notes file and the claims table. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
67. Under a row of real structures, draw a mirror row of plain shapes. Give each molecule one shape and one colour, and keep the order and the colours of the structures. The structure shows what the molecule is; the shape row shows what it does to the strands. Published figures of the method use ovals, a cut circle and a Y in this way (10.1371/journal.pbio.0040204, 10.1371/journal.pntd.0008044). (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
68. Draw a binding test whole. Show the binder on the line, both tags, the molecule that carries them and the particle with its binder. One binder with one tag does not show how the line gets its colour. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
69. Bring a product render to the level of a product photograph. Build one studio scene: every part on one white surface at true scale, soft boxes that the parts reflect, and contact shadows. Expose the surface to pure white, so that the picture edge leaves no frame (rule 62). Compare the render with a photograph of the same kind of part before it goes on a slide. (Owner, 2026-11-09, third correction on render quality.)
70. Draw a changed construction as one good variant, whole and in its place in the kit. Do not split the picture into "now" and "proposal", and do not put a "now" or "proposal" word in a name. Say the open points in the narration and write them in the notes file. A split picture shows two half designs, and the viewer does not learn which one is the product. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
71. Give the body of a device a neutral grey, and a metal part a metal finish. Do not colour a printed body yellow or another palette accent. Keep the accent colours for small parts that carry meaning: a cap, a cell, a lamp, a liquid. A yellow body pulls the eye from the sample and reads as a toy. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
72. Draw an electrical circuit for a Russian project as a schematic of the unified design documentation system: sheet frame and title block of ГОСТ 2.104, symbols of ГОСТ 2.721 to 2.759, reference designators of ГОСТ 2.710 (DA, VT, R, C, HL, SK, EK, XS, SA, GB, RK, RP), Russian text in a narrow italic face, an element list ПЭ3 on its own sheets. A hobby-style English schematic with vendor part names reads as a sketch, and a Russian engineer cannot pass it to a workshop. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
73. Model a device part by part before you render it: every electronic component as its own body on the board, every plate, cell, holder, switch and sensor in its place, and render the assembly and an exploded view in the same photoreal studio as the product render. A block drawn as a closed box hides what is inside, and a viewer cannot judge whether the design is real. Written sizes come from the data sheets; a size that is typical and not confirmed is named in the notes file. (Owner, 2026-11-09.)
74. Give a deck page no row of buttons. Show the place in the deck as a thin progress line, let a click or a key step on, and show the keys for a few seconds only (film field `controls: 'line'`). Make the deck print: one finished scene on each sheet (`cli.mjs pdf`). The last frame of a scene must tell its point to a reader who hears no narration. A button bar reads as a media player, and a slide that needs the voice is empty on paper. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
75. On a kit or process slide, put the scene in the vertical centre of the slide. Put the step texts in a zigzag, the first below the scene, the second above it, and so on. Join each text to its part with a bridge that is the still of a real molecule of the subject, rendered from a model. Put no panel behind a step text and no pool of tone behind the scene. A helix drawn as two crossing waves and a soft cloud under a picture both read as decoration. (Owner, 2026-11-10, twice.)
76. Draw a primer set as its oligos on the amplicon to one scale, with the full letters beside the map. One set takes three lines: forward primer, probe, reverse primer. On each line the oligo stands at its place on the amplicon and its letters stand in one column, from the 5' end to the 3' end, in regular weight. Show each modification as a round mark at its place. Colour a letter where the isolates of the alignment differ, and state the rule of the count in the notes. Put the name of the set and one plain line at the left. Use no tile over a letter and no hidden base. A row of tiles reads as censorship and as a table of cells. (Owner, 2026-11-10; published form: doi 10.3389/fmicb.2020.01015, figure 7.)
77. Build a paper consumable from its layers before you render it: backing card, each pad and the membrane at their real lengths and overlaps, a fibre relief on paper, the lines painted on the membrane with soft edges. Render it as a studio photograph in a three-quarter view. On a white page, put a soft pool of tone behind a white object, so that it keeps its edge. Name each layer on a leader that ends at the layer. A flat rectangle with three red bars is a sign of a strip and shows nothing of how it works. (Owner, 2026-11-10; published form: doi 10.3389/fmicb.2020.01015, figure 1.)
78. Let each entrance say what the part does. A part that arrives travels from where it comes; a strand that grows is drawn along its length; a bar grows from its zero; a number comes forward. Use the motion words of the deck engine for this (`slide`, `wipe`, `grow`, `zoom`, `blur`, moving keys, one loop). Keep at most one loop as the accent of a beat. A scene where every part only fades in has no motion that a viewer can read. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
79. Beside a venue sign, draw a partner mark as one flat tile in the ink colour of the partner, with its whole logo, all letters of it, and its name in its own typeface. Make the tile as high as the sign. Use no gradient, no pale pill and no outline, and do not set the name in the venue typeface. A logo with parts left out is a different logo. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
80. Lay a cost slide out as a bill. One row holds one item, the source of its price and the sum. Put the total under the rows and state the limit in one sentence beside it. Show an alternative product as a second line with its own price. A number with no source beside it reads as invented, and range bars on separate axes read as noise. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
81. Draw a mechanism as two illustrations at most, and let them hold all its stages at once. Put the still of each real structure, large, at the place where the protein acts on the molecule. Put a plain numeral, the name with its PDB entry and one spoken sentence beside each protein, and one key at the foot. A storyboard of rows with small structures at the side reads as a primitive scheme. (Owner, 2026-11-10; published form: doi 10.1038/nrm3805, figure 1.)
82. Take the model for a scheme from the Nature Reviews journals only. Draw DNA as they do: two thin rails joined by short rungs, with round ends. Let two strands part on a smooth bend. Draw a single strand as one calm rail. Give every mark a round shape: a dot for a tag, a dot for the unit with no base, a dot for the 3' block. Use no kinked polyline, no trapezoid bubble, no tick and no square mark. An angular scheme reads as a wiring diagram. (Owner, 2026-11-10, twice; published forms: doi 10.1038/nrm3805, figure 1, and doi 10.1038/s44222-022-00007-3, figures 1 and 2.)
83. Draw a particle or a bead as a sphere with a soft light from the upper left. Draw a label line as one thin line of the text colour. Use no step badge: a stage carries a plain numeral in the colour of its name. (Published form: doi 10.1038/s44222-022-00007-3, figure 2.)
84. Show a corrected recipe, bill or part already corrected. Put no verdict, no mark of a change and no thought about the change on the slide. The reason for each change goes into the notes file. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
85. Build the title page of a pitch deck after the deck "Arc Science, under the hood": a dark graphite page, the name very large at the left, one sentence in the first person under it, one large real object at the right, small caps labels at the top and much empty space. Set it in the typeface of the venue. The overview slide of the pitch4 deck is no model. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
86. Render a paper consumable on a real surface in studio light, with a contact shadow, the relief of each pad and lines that lie in the membrane. Compare it with a photograph of the same kind of part before you show it. (Owner, 2026-11-10; photograph: doi 10.3389/fmicb.2020.01015, figure 5.)
87. Show a device with an alternative as two products side by side, each with its picture and at most three facts. An exploded view with a line to every part reads as a parts list. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
88. Fill the slide. A table or a picture that ends at half the height of the slide gets a larger size, a wider row pitch or a real picture beside it. A hollow lower half reads as an unfinished slide. (Owner, 2026-10-12, 2026-10-20 and 2026-11-10.)
89. Set a label that names a part of a picture in semibold. Keep the paragraphs of the slide in regular weight. A reader then tells a label from the text at a glance. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
90. Put no sentence under the name of a column or of a block. The name is the header; what the thing does is said aloud and kept in the notes. This is the rule on subtitles, and it holds inside the slide too. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
91. Give each letter of a sequence a cell of at least 0.68 of the type size: 15 px for 22 px type. Measure the widest letter of the face first; in HSE Sans, G and C are 0.77 of the size. Letters in a narrower cell crowd their neighbours. Check the right margin after you widen the cells. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
92. Set a row of sequence letters at one pitch. The cell is as wide as the widest letter of the face. A mark on a letter is a rounded cell, one letter wide. Do not use a circle or a pill for it. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
93. Keep a paragraph at least 40 px away from a picture and from a label of a picture. Move the picture or cut a label before you let them touch. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
94. Draw an arrow from the text of a step to the part it describes. A step text with no arrow leaves the viewer to guess. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
95. Set a brand name in the face of its logo. Put the product name at the bottom right corner of a slide. Write the word "by" of a mark in lower case. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
96. Give the name of a protein with no database entry in it. Keep the PDB entry in the claims and the notes. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
97. Make the gaps over and under each drawing of a row equal, and the same in every column. Draw the columns to one height first. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
98. Draw a structural formula from a list of atoms and bonds, and check that list against the intended molecule with RDKit. Set the atom letters in cells, at 18 px or more for 24 px bonds. Name the bond that breaks on the bond itself. (Owner, 2026-11-10.)
99. Put no paragraph on a slide that already holds two formulas, a reaction scheme and two renders. Use captions of 28 px, and say the sentences aloud. (Deck check, 2026-11-10.)

## Common faults

These are the faults that the rules above catch most often:

- a box with a number in it where a mark on an axis belongs (rule 29);
- a bar for a mean with no points and no error (rule 31);
- a box nested inside another box, or stage strips and badges wrapping inner cards (rules 40 and 41);
- a divider, lifeline or arrow cutting through a text label (rules 46 and 55);
- hand-coded SVG bonds or circle-and-stick cartoons in place of RDKit `MolDraw2D` or a 3D render (rules 51 and 52);
- a chain of three to six boxes with one to three words each and no object;
- a generic icon on every box;
- a blob where a real object belongs;
